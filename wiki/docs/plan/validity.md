# Is the test any good?

A session can feel great and tell you nothing. This page is how to tell the difference.

The short version: **a playtest is only worth something if it could have gone badly.** If the
setup made a bad result impossible, you learned nothing, however pleasant the evening was.

---

## The six-question check

Run this after each session. If you can't answer yes to at least four, treat the results as a
nice evening rather than as data.

<div class="grid cards" markdown>

-   :material-account-question: **Were they strangers?**

    ---

    Friends fill in your gaps without noticing. They finish your sentences, forgive a confusing
    rule, and tell you it was fun because they like you.

    **Strangers at a library are worth five sessions with your regular group.**

-   :material-account-voice: **Did you keep quiet?**

    ---

    If you explained the rules before they read the table tent, you tested your explanation, not
    the page. At the convention you will not be sitting next to every table.

-   :material-timer: **Did you actually time it?**

    ---

    "It felt quick" is not a number. A stopwatch is the difference between an impression and a
    measurement.

-   :material-pencil: **Did you write it down at the table?**

    ---

    Memory smooths everything. An hour later the hesitations are gone and only the good bits
    remain.

-   :material-repeat: **Has this happened twice?**

    ---

    One table is an anecdote. Two tables doing the same thing is a pattern. **Don't change a
    rule on the strength of one session.**

-   :material-comment-question: **Did you ask neutrally?**

    ---

    "That was fun, right?" gets you a yes every time. "What confused you?" gets you the truth,
    because it presumes something did.

</div>

## The one that people miss

!!! warning "If Jeff narrates every session, Jeff is what's being tested"

    An enthusiastic Narrator carries a table. They cover an unclear rule with a good improvised
    answer, and nobody notices the rule was unclear.

    That's wonderful for the evening and useless for the game.

    **At least twice before the convention, somebody else should narrate** &mdash; ideally
    someone who has never run a game, working only from the printed kit. If they can, the rules
    are teachable. If they can't, the rules need Jeff in the room, which does not scale to a
    convention hall.

    This is the single most informative test in the programme, and it's the one that feels
    least comfortable to run.

---

## Signs the session didn't really test anything

- **Nobody asked a single question.** Either it's genuinely clear, or they were being polite. Ask directly which it was.
- **You answered a rules question before anyone read the rules.** The clock effectively hadn't started.
- **You changed three things since last time.** Now you can't tell which one helped. Change one thing at a time.
- **Everyone rolled the same Skill all night.** That's usually a scenario problem, not a rules result &mdash; see [test 3](tests.md).
- **It ran long and you called it a win because they enjoyed it.** They had nowhere to be. At a convention they do.
- **You didn't photograph the sheets.** Then it didn't happen.

---

## What counts as enough

You don't need a research programme. You need enough that you're not guessing:

| Question | Enough is |
| --- | --- |
| Is the game learnable? | 3 sessions with strangers, all under 15 minutes to first roll |
| Is a rule unclear? | The same question asked at 2 different tables |
| Does a fix work? | The question stops being asked at the next 2 tables |
| Is it convention-ready? | 2 timed 40-minute runs that reached an ending |

!!! tip "When results disagree"

    If one table loves something and another is baffled by it, the difference is almost always
    **who narrated**, not who played. Note the Narrator on every session brief and that pattern
    becomes visible.
